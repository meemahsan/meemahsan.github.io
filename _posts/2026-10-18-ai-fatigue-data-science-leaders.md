I lead data science and AI teams for a living.

I spend my days thinking about machine learning, generative AI, agents, data, experimentation, automation, and how all of those things can create measurable business value.

And lately, I have found myself thinking something I didn't expect:

**I am tired of AI.**

Not tired of the technology. Not skeptical about its potential. And certainly not ready to stop building with it.

I am tired of trying to keep up with it.

There is a difference.

## The Pace Has Become Part of the Job

For most of my career in data science, learning was continuous but manageable.

A new modeling technique would become popular. A new Python library would emerge. Cloud platforms evolved. MLOps matured. New architectures appeared.

You learned. You experimented. You figured out where something fit into your stack.

AI today feels fundamentally different.

Every week seems to bring another model, another agent framework, another benchmark, another coding assistant, another protocol, another announcement telling us that the way we worked six months ago is suddenly obsolete.

As a practitioner, that's exciting.

As a leader, it can be exhausting.

Because I am not simply asking:

**“Should I learn this?”**

I am asking:

**“Does my team need to learn this?”**

**“Does this change our architecture?”**

**“Is this mature enough for production?”**

**“Is there actually a business problem here?”**

**“Should we change something we just built?”**

And perhaps the most dangerous question:

**“Are we falling behind?”**

That last question creates a tremendous amount of unnecessary anxiety in technology organizations.

## From FOMO to Organizational FOMO

AI FOMO isn't limited to individuals anymore.

Organizations experience it too.

Someone sees an impressive demo and asks why we aren't doing it.

A new model launches and suddenly an existing roadmap feels outdated.

A competitor announces an AI capability and the immediate reaction is: *We need an agent.*

The pressure becomes especially interesting for data science leaders because we sit between two very different worlds.

On one side is the extraordinary speed of AI research and technology.

On the other is the reality of enterprise systems.

Real data.

Real users.

Security reviews.

Governance.

Integration.

Change management.

Production reliability.

And ultimately, measurable business outcomes.

A prototype can be built in a weekend.

Transforming a business process is something entirely different.

That distinction is becoming increasingly important.

## Experimentation Is Not the Same as Progress

One of the lessons I am learning as an AI leader is that the ability to experiment faster does not necessarily mean we should experiment with everything.

This is surprisingly difficult.

AI makes starting incredibly cheap.

You can create a prototype in hours. You can test a new model quickly. You can spin up an agent, connect tools and demonstrate something impressive before lunch.

The bottleneck is no longer always our ability to build.

Increasingly, the bottleneck is our ability to decide **what deserves to be built.**

That requires judgment.

And judgment becomes harder when you're constantly consuming new information.

At some point, keeping up with AI can become its own job.

That's where curiosity quietly turns into fatigue.

## AI Fatigue Can Affect the People Most Excited About AI

We sometimes interpret fatigue as resistance.

I think that's a mistake.

Some of the people experiencing the most AI fatigue may actually be the people who are most engaged with it.

They are reading the papers.

Testing the models.

Trying the coding assistants.

Watching demonstrations.

Building agents.

Following new frameworks.

Rethinking their architecture.

And simultaneously trying to do their actual jobs.

The result isn't necessarily rejection of AI.

It's cognitive overload.

Research is beginning to treat AI fatigue as a distinct phenomenon involving cognitive overload, emotional strain, behavioral disengagement and even physical exhaustion.

That resonates with what I see happening in technology organizations.

The problem isn't that people don't want to learn.

The problem is that **there is no natural stopping point anymore.**

## Leadership Has to Change

I've started thinking differently about what it means to lead a data science team in this environment.

A few years ago, part of my responsibility was making sure my team stayed current.

Today, I think an equally important responsibility is deciding what they **don't** need to chase.

Not every new model deserves an evaluation.

Not every framework deserves a proof of concept.

Not every AI announcement deserves a roadmap change.

Not every problem needs an agent.

That requires leaders to become filters.

Our job isn't simply to bring more AI into the organization.

It is to absorb some of the noise so our teams can focus.

## I’m Trying to Follow a Few Simple Rules

**Start with the problem, not the technology.**

Instead of asking, “Where can we use agents?” ask, “What problem are our users struggling with?”

Sometimes the answer will be an agent.

Sometimes it will be traditional machine learning.

Sometimes it will be better data.

Sometimes it will be a simple rule.

That is still good data science.

**Separate exploration from commitment.**

Teams should have room to experiment without every successful demo becoming a production initiative.

A prototype answers: *Can this work?*

It does not automatically answer: *Should we build and support this?*

**Create a technology filter.**

Instead of asking an entire team to keep up with everything, distribute exploration. Let different people investigate areas deeply and bring back what matters.

The organization learns without requiring every individual to consume the entire AI ecosystem.

**Protect deep thinking.**

Ironically, one of my biggest concerns about AI productivity is that we may use every minute AI saves us to create more work.

If AI saves two hours and we fill those two hours with additional meetings, prompts, dashboards, agents and decisions, we haven't necessarily improved work.

We've just accelerated it.

Some of that reclaimed time should go back to thinking.

**Measure outcomes, not AI activity.**

Number of agents built is not a business metric.

Number of prompts isn't either.

Neither is the number of AI experiments running across an organization.

The questions I care about are much more traditional:

Did we increase revenue?

Did we save someone meaningful time?

Did we improve a decision?

Did we create a better customer or employee experience?

Did we solve a problem that mattered?

If the answer is yes, AI was useful.

If the answer is no, it doesn't matter how sophisticated the architecture was.

## Maybe Keeping Up Is the Wrong Goal

This may be the biggest mindset shift for me.

I don't think it is possible to completely “keep up with AI” anymore.

And I'm increasingly convinced that trying to do so is the wrong objective.

The goal isn't to know every new model.

The goal isn't to test every new framework.

The goal isn't to react to every announcement.

The goal is to build enough understanding to recognize when a meaningful capability emerges—and enough discipline to ignore the rest.

For data science leaders, that may become one of our most important skills.

Because the next generation of strong AI organizations won't necessarily be the ones experimenting with the most AI.

They may be the ones that know **where to pay attention, where to invest, and when to say no.**

I am still incredibly optimistic about AI.

I still think we are at the beginning of a major transformation in how knowledge work gets done.

But being optimistic about AI doesn't require being constantly excited about every development in AI.

Sometimes good leadership means giving yourself—and your team—permission to stop chasing the feed, put down the latest demo, and get back to solving the problem in front of you.

Maybe that's not falling behind.

Maybe that's maturity.
